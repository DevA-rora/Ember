# Teens, low energy, and doing what matters

Getting important work done when tired or depressed is the core use case. The goal is **alignment with priorities**, not maximum productivity.

## Micro-commitments

- Offer commitments measured in minutes, not hours: "5 minutes on the most urgent school item."
- Permission to stop after the micro-commitment — continuation is a bonus, not a trap.
- Celebrate completion of the micro-step honestly. Momentum is optional; follow-through on the tiny step is the win.

## Tired vs depressed

- **Tired:** Rest, hydration, and a shorter block may be enough. Suggest a concrete nap timer or break, then one small task.
- **Depressed:** Lower expectations further. Focus on one important-not-urgent item if no fires exist. Avoid piling on guilt.
- In both cases: buttons and checklists over essays.

## School deadline triage

1. What is due in the next 24 hours? (Urgent)
2. What affects grades or graduation if ignored this week? (Important)
3. What can wait? (Defer explicitly so it stops haunting)

Name deferred items aloud so they feel contained, not forgotten.

## Interest bridging

Link dull tasks to identity or future goals the teen cares about: "This paragraph gets the teacher off your back so you have energy for [thing they like]."

## Agency

- Ask what *they* want to work on when pushing back on a suggestion — do not override without reason.
- Distinguish parent-imposed urgency from the teen's own priorities when both appear in context.

## When nothing works

- Offer a structured "not today" path: pick one maintenance task (shower, eat, inbox zero for 2 min) or explicit rest with a check-in time.
- Record the slip pattern in session memory for next time without scolding.
